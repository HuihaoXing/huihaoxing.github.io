Case study, analyze and grow

Ads got 44% cheaper. Should we spend more?

A B2B food brand ran a four-week search-ads pilot. I tuned bids and search terms until each click cost 44% less. By the dashboard it was working, so before touching the budget I checked what the clicks actually did.

Role
Sole analyst and engineer
Stack
SQL, Python, statsmodels, scikit-learn
Data
First-party site events, ad click IDs, UTM tags
Privacy
Every figure is a rate or a ratio. Volumes, dates and spend stay private.

01

What the dashboard said.

Cost per click fell every week, 44% by week four. The obvious move was a bigger budget. But a cheaper click is only good news if it turns into something, and the dashboard could not tell me that.

02

What those visitors did.

I joined every ad click ID and UTM tag to site sessions in SQL and counted who took a next step.

Visit
One session. Bots removed by user agent, our own traffic removed by IP.
Ad visitor
The first page view carries an ad click ID or a paid-search tag.
Next step
A contact, wholesale, product or sample button, or opening the wholesale form.
2.7%of ad visitors took a next step (95% interval 1.6 to 4.5)
17%of everyone else (11.2 to 24.1)

A correction I made laterMy first count included the cookie banner’s “Got it” button and put everyone else at 19%. Removing it changed the numbers, not the decision.

03

A fairer comparison.

Every ad visitor landed on the wholesale page. Most other visitors came in through the homepage, and many already knew the brand. So I compared visitors on the same page.

2.7%of ad visitors
7.3%of others on the wholesale page (2.5 to 19.4)

The gap shrinks, but the ad rate is still less than half.

What this does not claimThe difference is not the causal effect of the ads: people who find a brand on their own are different people. The causal check is a holdout by region or by week; the pilot did not justify one.

04

Why the clicks were cheap.

Two broad-match search terms brought 71% of the ad clicks. The people behind them were looking for a general food distributor, not a specialty maker. Searches for the product itself, the people actually looking for us, were 7% of clicks.

Put together, it took about 37 ad clicks to earn one next step.

Why the terms have lettersThe keyword research belongs to the business, so search terms appear here as A and B.

Decision

Pause the broad ads.

A cheaper click does not rescue a rate this low, and the other channels were already doing better without spend. If we buy search again, it will be product-name searches only, with a holdout so the result can be read as cause and effect.

Then

Channel or visitor? Mostly where they came from.

To learn what drives a next step on the homepage, I trained a gradient-boosted model on what is known at landing plus the first 30 seconds of behaviour.

0.69ROC AUC, source alone
0.69ROC AUC, every feature

The extra features added nothing; where a visitor came from carried the signal. Organic-search visitors took a next step at 26.9%, referral visitors at 6.5%, about four times apart, so acquisition moved toward organic search.

Three leaks I removed first

  • Largest Contentful Paint: the browser reports it after the visitor interacts, so it encoded the outcome.
  • Sessions that landed on the wholesale page: they cannot click the homepage button.
  • Behaviour after the first click: early signals are cut strictly before it, at 30 seconds.

CorrectedWith cookie-banner clicks counted, the model scored 0.75 and 0.77. Corrected, both are 0.69. The conclusion held.

And

The A/B test I sized, and did not launch.

Before building a new homepage I sized the test: two-sided, alpha 0.05, power 0.80, looking for a 30% lift.

2,458sessions needed on the corrected 13.7% baseline
17.8%the rate a new page would have to reach

At the time, on a 14.2% baseline, the answer was 2,358. Either way it was more than the site could send in a reasonable window, so I documented the design instead of launching a test that could only end inconclusive.
